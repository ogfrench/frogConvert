# Narrative craft for different presentation jobs

Start with what this audience needs to understand, question, experience or decide. A presentation can be an argument, a factual account, an operating review, a demonstration or a workshop. Choose a suitable progression rather than a compulsory three-act or problem/solution formula.

## Structures worth knowing

| Task | Useful reasoning | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decision or investment | Options, decision criteria, supporting evidence, trade-offs, downside and ask | Hiding the most important objection |
| Research findings | Population and method, comparisons, findings, caveats and open questions | Treating observations as mandatory recommendations |
| Operating review | Current state, change, ownership, constraints, decisions and next steps | Inflated vision language instead of operational facts |
| Workshop | Shared context, useful stimuli, questions, activity and synthesis | Forcing every slide to state a thesis |
| Keynote or portfolio | Story, examples, pacing, visual contrast and memorable reveals | Filling every beat with consulting-style headers |

These are examples, not prescribed outlines or slide counts. A single page may suffice; a complex topic can require a much longer treatment.

## Build an argument only when one is needed

Distinguish **thesis** (the claim), **premises** (reasons), **evidence** (observations), **implication** (why it matters) and **ask** (choice or action). These can be implicit in a short deck and do not need separate artifacts.

*Fictional pilot:* Four of six locations missed a defined service target. Two met it. A defensible story is "Readiness differs across locations; investigate why before broad rollout." The leap "The solution fails everywhere" ignores contradictory evidence; "Training caused the failures" assigns an untested cause.

A skeptical audience may need to understand the strongest contrary observation early. A technical audience may prefer method and limitations before interpretation. An executive may need the choice and relevant trade-offs before a detailed appendix. Do not mistake one audience preference for a universal slide order.

## Rhythm and transitions

Each slide should add a meaningful observation, question, comparison, mechanism or decision. Repeat a composition when it makes comparisons easier, and vary it when the reasoning changes. A dense evidence view can sit beside a quieter synthesis, but neither sparseness nor variety is inherently virtuous.

Check the line of reasoning at three levels: titles, salient visuals and supporting evidence. They should not tell contradictory stories. A recommendation should not be smuggled into a neutral data report simply to create a dramatic ending.

## Collaborate through work

If the brief is sufficient, draft or make the deck. Preserve an approved storyline unless asked to change it. Present alternatives only for a meaningful unresolved decision. For high-stakes work, challenge the weakest material inference, important alternative explanations and the cost of the proposed decision. Do not introduce a mandatory interview or adversarial ceremony for ordinary work.
