# Action titles: the slide's supported point

An **action title** names a finding, implication or recommendation rather than simply naming a topic. "Action" describes what the headline does for the argument, not an obligation to use an imperative verb. It is a strong default for evidence-led, executive and persuasive slides, not a required format on every slide.

All examples below are fictional and illustrate editing techniques, not claims about a real organization.

| Type | Example | Why it works or does not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Topic | Customer retention | Names an area but withholds the point |
| Finding | Most observed cancellations occurred during the first month | Makes a testable claim, if the actual cohort data supports it |
| Interpretation | Early onboarding may be contributing to cancellations | Signals a hypothesis, not a proven cause |
| Recommendation | Test first-month onboarding changes before expanding the program | Proposes an action; the evidence does not itself prove the outcome |
| Decision request | Fund a limited test of the revised onboarding flow | Specifies the requested choice |

## Derive the headline from the evidence

- Ask what the slide establishes, suggests, or asks the audience to decide. Distinguish observation from inference and recommendation.
- Name the actor, comparison, direction and timeframe when they make the point clearer, but do not cram every dimension into the title.
- Match confidence to the source. A pilot is not automatically evidence of general impact. Correlation is not causation.
- The headline and the visual must agree. A title about a growing gap needs a valid comparable trend; a title about a bottleneck needs evidence of the bottleneck.
- Read headlines in sequence when the deck argues toward a decision. A factual report may instead group independent findings without an artificial thesis.

## Worked edits

**Vague:** "Key takeaways"  
**Finding, if supported:** "Two approval steps account for most observed delays"  
**What must be shown:** The measured delay for each step, source, units and period. If durations were not measured, use "Approval includes two handoffs" instead.

**Overclaim:** "Our new workflow doubles productivity"  
**Defensible, if supported:** "The pilot team completed 18 of 25 tasks, versus 14 of 25 in a separate cohort"  
**What remains unknown:** Different cohorts alone do not prove the workflow caused the difference. If comparability is limited, say so visibly.

**Unsupported recommendation disguised as fact:** "Automation will remove waiting time"  
**Proposal:** "Test whether automated routing reduces queue time"  
**Why:** The mechanism is plausible; the result is not established.

Covers, chapter titles, workshop prompts, quotations, visual reveals and photography can work better with a label, question or no headline. Strong writing is not a quota of complete-sentence claims. Avoid making every title use the same contrast, triad, dramatic fragment or declarative cadence.
